P1 — The Fuel Shock Panel (Flagship Study)

Core question: How do coping, wellbeing and trust in the state change over the fuel shock cycle — and how does that path differ between public-sector, formal-private and informal households?

Design

Element Detail
Method CATI phone panel (rapid-response)
Sample 1,500–2,500 households, stratified by sector: government / formal private (incl. RMG) / informal non-farm (incl. transport) / farm
Coverage Dhaka, Chattogram, Gazipur/Narayanganj (RMG belt), Rangpur & Rajshahi (Boro/irrigation), one coastal district
Waves W0 now → W1 Nov → W2 Jan → W3 Mar → W4 Jun 2027
Each wave 15–20 min interview
Budget ~$35–60k for 5 waves (~$4–6 per completed CATI interview, plus survey-firm management fee and Tk 50–100 mobile top-up incentives per wave)

Core modules (each wave)

  • Spending cuts — ranked across 12 items
  • Food insecurity (FIES 8-item scale)
  • Transport mode, time and cost
  • Kerosene/lighting and children’s study hours
  • Intra-household allocation — who ate less, who gave up mobility
  • Relative deprivation — comparisons to government employees
  • Borrowing (NGO, shopkeeper credit, kin, mobile-money)
  • Blame attribution and trust in institutions
  • Family Card receipt — whether targeted compensation is actually reaching households
  • Protest willingness and participation

Embedded experiment (W0)

Before asking whether the hike is “acceptable,” respondents are randomly assigned one of four framings: - (a) No explanation - (b) “Global oil prices doubled” - (c) “The government keeps Tk 32/litre as tax while losing Tk 69/litre on diesel” - (d) “Government employees just received a pay rise of up to 142%”

This is a moral-economy test: does perceived fairness predict acceptance better than the absolute size of the shock?

The Family Card question — where P1 intersects the technocratic-fix critique directly

The government’s stated mitigation tool is the Family Card — a digital safety-net database covering ~1 crore households, launched March 2026. P1 tracks, wave by wave, who in our sample receives it and who doesn’t — with particular attention to documentation barriers, mobile-money access, and local political gatekeeping. The working hypothesis is that the card’s coverage, even if administratively correct, will leave out the households most exposed to the shock: informal workers with no fixed employer, households without NID or mobile-money accounts, and those outside the political networks that historically governed Union Parishad distributions.

A compensation mechanism that looks well-designed on paper (targeted, digital, scalable) can still function as a gesture if the structural conditions for exclusion are reproduced inside the delivery system. P1 will generate evidence on whether that is happening.

What we are asking from reviewers

  • Does the Family Card module in P1 ask the right questions to surface exclusion — or does it inadvertently take the targeting mechanism at face value?
  • Are there structural conditions — documentation, geography, gender, political economy — that we missed?

Recent Context (September 2026 Perspective)

The significance of P1’s sector-stratified design is underscored by the events of September 2026. Within a 48-hour window, the government gazetted a National Pay Scale raising public-sector wages by up to 142%, followed by a flat Tk 20/litre fuel hike. This creates a stark distributional natural experiment: public employees are insulated, while informal workers (approx. 85% of employment) face the unmitigated shock of diesel-driven inflation and direct kerosene costs. Furthermore, the standard media framing focuses on octane affordability, obscuring the “recognition failure” where off-grid, kerosene-dependent households bear the most regressive burden without any targeted mitigation. P1’s wave-based tracking is uniquely positioned to capture this real-time distributional architecture.
