P5 — Cash or Cheap Fuel? (Conjoint Experiment)

Core question: Do Bangladeshis prefer universal cheap fuel or targeted cash? How do trust in the state, past safety-net experience, and perceived corruption shape that preference?

Design

A conjoint survey experiment, either embedded in P1’s W1 or run independently (n ~ 2,000).

Respondents choose between policy packages that vary across five attributes:

Attribute Levels
Pump price Tk 115 / Tk 135 / Tk 150
Monthly compensation None / Tk 500 / Tk 1,000
Targeting Universal / Family Card / Employer-based
Delivery mechanism bKash/Nagad / Union Parishad / NGO
Who pays Duty cut / Government budget / BPC borrowing

Moderators: sector of employment, prior safety-net receipt, institutional trust, partisanship.

Budget

~$8–15k standalone, and small if embedded in P1’s W1.

Why it matters

This design directly tests whether people’s preferences for compensation track the targeting mechanism itself — i.e., whether Family Card delivery is trusted, or whether the Union Parishad/NGO route retains legitimacy that a digital government system hasn’t yet earned. It also tests whether the policy frame (duty cut vs. price hike vs. direct transfer) changes what feels acceptable, even at the same net fiscal cost.

P5 adds Bangladesh to a growing comparative literature on fuel subsidy reform preferences (Iran 2010, Indonesia, Nigeria 2023) and asks a locally specific question: given Bangladesh’s history with leaky safety nets and politically gatekept distributions, does a trust deficit make targeted cash politically unviable even when it is economically preferable?

What we are asking from reviewers

  • Are the delivery mechanism levels realistic given what you know about how safety-net programmes have worked on the ground?
  • Are there structural conditions — documentation, geography, gender, political economy — that the conjoint design misses or understates?

Recent Context (September 2026 Perspective)

The debate over fuel subsidies vs. targeted cash is acutely relevant in light of the September 2026 fuel hike. Current mitigation discourse focuses almost entirely on the Family Card digital safety-net as a response to the diesel/commuter cost shock. However, this framing completely overlooks the kerosene burden borne by off-grid, poorest households. By testing preferences for targeting mechanisms and who pays (duty cut vs. direct transfer), this conjoint experiment will reveal if the actual victims of the price hike—those suffering from recognition injustice—trust the Family Card, or if the public prefers a universal approach due to a fundamental trust deficit in state delivery mechanisms.
