P2 — Charging the City — an ethnography of the e-rickshaw economy under load shedding

Question. How do battery-rickshaw drivers, garage owners, charging-point operators and local power brokers govern an illegal energy economy? How does the fuel hike redistribute risk and income across that chain?

Design. - Sites: 3 neighbourhoods in Dhaka (one core, one peri-urban, one where a crackdown or relocation is active) and 1 district town. - Methods: - 4–6 months of participant observation at garages and charging points - 60–80 life-history interviews with drivers, sampled for ex-farmers, ex-RMG workers, returned migrants and women in the business (very rare, so an important case) - driver daily diaries (earnings, fares, charging costs, rent, “costs to police or fixers”, hours) over 8 weeks, collected via WhatsApp voice notes, with a small stipend - mapping of charging points to cross-check the reported 48,136 illegal vs 3,300 authorised - Mixed-method add-on: a structured survey of 400 drivers at charging points to estimate the share who moved into rickshaws from each prior occupation.

Outputs. An ethnographic paper on informal energy governance, a policy note on legalisation and metering (lever #5 in the README), and a photo-essay (with consent).

Budget. ~$15–30k (researcher stipend, RA, diaries, transcription, translation).

What we are asking from reviewers. Do the three Dhaka site types (core, peri-urban, active-crackdown) plus one district town cover the range of governance arrangements in the charging economy, or are there arrangements — rural charging points, cross-district battery/parts supply chains — the design is missing? Does collecting driver diaries over WhatsApp voice notes bias the sample toward drivers who already have smartphone access and some stability, undercounting the most precarious operators?

Recent context (September 2026 perspective)

The Tk 20/litre hike (Tk 165 octane, Tk 135 diesel) widens the operating-cost wedge between petrol and battery three-wheelers from roughly 15:1 to about 17:1 — a petrol autorickshaw now costs an estimated Tk 11/km in fuel, against Tk 0.4–0.8/km for a battery rickshaw drawing from one of Dhaka’s ~48,136 illegal charging points, where the marginal electricity cost to the operator is close to zero. That is the mechanism this study is built to observe directly: as the fuel shock pushes more drivers and passengers toward the battery fleet, the informal charging economy absorbs more load, more revenue and more risk (crackdowns, extortion payments, grid strain), and the August 2026 solar-charging mandate is too slow to substitute for it this winter. P2’s site selection (including a neighbourhood with an active crackdown or relocation) and driver sampling (reportedly ~21% of the battery-rickshaw fleet is ex-farmers) are designed to catch this intensification as it happens, not reconstruct it after the fact.
