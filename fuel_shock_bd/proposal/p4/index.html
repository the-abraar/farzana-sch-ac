P4 — Who Gets the Blame? — computational analysis of public discourse on the fuel shock

Question. How are responsibility and fairness framed in Bangla-language public discourse about fuel between 2022 and 2026? Which frames predict offline protest?

Design. - Corpus: - Bangla and English news (Prothom Alo, Daily Star, TBS, Jugantor, Samakal, Kaler Kantho) - public Facebook pages and groups, YouTube comments on news clips, with platform terms and data-access rules followed - parliamentary debates - covering the 2022 hike, the April, June and September 2026 hikes, and the upcoming October–December 2026 events - Methods: - Bangla NLP: topic models, a frame classifier (global market / government failure / corruption-syndicate / India / fairness-pay scale / apology-empathy), sentiment - event data on protests (coded from news, ACLED where available) - time-series linking frame intensity to protest events and hike dates - Validation: human-coded sample (inter-coder reliability >=0.7), plus a check against the P1 survey blame questions (the same framing categories).

Budget. Low: ~$5–15k (RA coders, compute). The most feasible solo or remote project, and a strong companion paper to P1.

What we are asking from reviewers. Does the six-category frame classifier (global market / government failure / corruption-syndicate / India / fairness-pay scale / apology-empathy) match how blame and fairness actually get argued in Bangla-language discourse, or is there a frame specific to this hike — the BPC/LC financing crisis itself — that deserves its own category rather than folding into “government failure”? Is coding protest events from news coverage alone reliable enough, given that small or local protests are the ones most likely to go unreported, biasing the frame-to-protest link toward already-visible, larger events?

Recent context (September 2026 perspective)

The two-day gap between the National Pay Scale gazette (+142% for roughly 33 lakh public employees) and the Tk 20/litre fuel hike is itself a discourse event worth its own row in the corpus: it is the clearest real-world test yet of the fairness-pay-scale frame P4’s classifier is built to detect, and lets the study check whether that frame’s intensity in the days after 19–21 September predicts protest activity the way 2022 diesel-hike coverage did. It also sharpens the validation check against P1: if households in P1’s W0 blame module cite the pay-scale contrast unprompted, that is a direct read on whether the frame P4 tracks in public discourse is the same one operating in private household judgment.
