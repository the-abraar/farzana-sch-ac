P3 — Boro Decisions — farmers, water sellers and the irrigation cost shock

Question. How do diesel costs, supply uncertainty and a frozen procurement price change farmers’ decisions about how much Boro to plant? Who in the agrarian hierarchy absorbs the cost?

Design. - Sites: 6 villages in 2–3 upazilas, in Rangpur, Rajshahi and Mymensingh (diesel shallow-tube-well heavy), with 1 grid/solar-irrigation village as a contrast. - Data collection: - a survey before planting (Nov) and at harvest (May), n~600 plots or farmers: tenure, planned vs actual area, irrigation arrangement and price, borrowing, household migration - interviews with pump owners and water sellers about how they set prices - focus groups with women in farming households on food and labour trade-offs - Administrative data: Department of Agricultural Extension (DAE) Boro area targets vs actuals, and BADC diesel/pump data if accessible.

Why it’s important. This is the channel most likely to turn into a 2027 food-price and forex problem. Almost no one is watching it yet (see §4c of the README).

Budget. ~$20–35k.

What we are asking from reviewers. Does a sample of 6 villages across Rangpur, Rajshahi and Mymensingh capture enough variation in diesel-irrigation markets — including areas where diesel is already selling Tk 15–80/litre above the regulated price — or does the design need a site in a more acutely shortage-prone area (char land, Barind tract) to see the full range of the acreage response? Is one grid/solar-irrigation village enough to serve as a counterfactual, or does that comparison need its own expanded sample?

Recent context (September 2026 perspective)

Diesel already covers roughly 70% of Bangladesh’s ~15 lakh shallow tube wells, and the Tk 20/litre hike lands squarely ahead of the November–December planting decisions this study is built to capture: baseline irrigation cost is Tk 3,500–4,000/bigha, of which diesel is roughly Tk 2,400, and this hike alone adds an estimated Tk 1,070 crore in aggregate irrigation cost to the coming Boro crop (about Tk 1.0–1.4 per kg of production cost) — while the government has held the Boro procurement price at its old level. That combination, rising input cost against a frozen output price, is the margin-compression mechanism P3’s survey is designed to trace from farmer decision through to acreage and yield. It is also unlikely to be the last increase before planting: BPC’s import-financing crunch in October makes a further hike or duty cut probable, which could land inside P3’s November baseline window rather than after it.
